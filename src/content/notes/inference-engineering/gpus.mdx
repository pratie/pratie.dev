The last chapter ended with two limits: how fast a chip can calculate, and how fast it can fetch. So the next step is to look at the chips, and that means running into a wall of names. H100. L40. B200. Hopper. Blackwell. MIG. NVLink.

Almost all of it reduces to three numbers and a naming scheme.

## Why a GPU and not a CPU?

A CPU is a handful of very capable workers. Each can do complicated, branching work, one step after another.

A GPU is tens of thousands of simple workers who all do the same small thing at the same moment. The book calls GPUs throughput machines, and that is the right picture.

Running a model is almost nothing except multiplying and adding enormous grids of numbers. Every multiplication is independent of its neighbours. That is exactly the kind of work you can hand to ten thousand simple workers at once, and exactly the kind a few clever ones are wasted on.

## Inside the chip

The workers come in groups. An H100 has 132 of these groups, called **streaming multiprocessors**, and each holds a few kinds of worker. The kind that matters here is the **Tensor Core**. It is built for one instruction: multiply two grids of numbers and add the result to a third. That instruction is most of what running a model consists of, so when a spec sheet quotes compute for AI, it is counting Tensor Cores.

Memory comes in two kinds as well. The big one is the GPU's main memory, the tens or hundreds of gigabytes where the weights live. It sits next to the chip, not on it. On the chip itself are small and very fast scratchpads called caches. An H100 has 256 KB for each group of workers and 50 MB shared between all of them. Anything a worker touches has to be carried from the big memory into those scratchpads first.

That is the physical reason behind [the last chapter](/inference-engineering/prefill-and-decode). Weights are measured in gigabytes and the fast memory in megabytes, so the weights can never stay on the chip. They stream across it, all of them, once for every pass.

## The three numbers

**Compute** is how fast the chip does arithmetic, in FLOPS: floating-point operations per second. Datacenter GPUs do trillions, so the unit you see is teraFLOPS. This is the limit for prefill, and for image and video models.

Two traps when reading this number. Spec sheets quote a "sparse" figure that assumes half the numbers are zero and can be skipped. Normal inference is not like that, so read the "dense" one. And the figure roughly doubles each time you halve the number of bits per number, so only compare two GPUs at the same precision.

**Memory size** is how much the GPU can hold, in gigabytes. GPU memory is called VRAM. The model's weights have to fit, with room left over for the working memory that attention needs, the KV cache. The book's rule of thumb is at least half as much again as the weights. If the weights do not fit, the model fails to load. If the headroom runs out, requests fail halfway through.

**Memory bandwidth** is how fast the chip can pull data out of that memory. This is the limit for decode, as [the last chapter](/inference-engineering/prefill-and-decode) showed. If you want more tokens per second per user, this is the number to shop for.

Here are four GPUs you will hear about, with the numbers from the book's tables.

| GPU | Compute, 8-bit | Memory | Bandwidth | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| L4 | 242 teraFLOPS | 24 GB | 300 GB/s | Small models, cheaply |
| H100 | 1,979 teraFLOPS | 80 GB | 3,350 GB/s | The workhorse |
| H200 | 1,979 teraFLOPS | 141 GB | 4,800 GB/s | Bigger models, faster decode |
| B200 | about 5,000 teraFLOPS | 192 GB | up to 8,000 GB/s | The largest models, video |

Look at the H100 and the H200. Same compute. The H200 has more memory and moves it faster. From the last chapter you can predict what that buys: it reads a prompt no quicker, and it writes the answer faster.

## Decoding the names

A name like B200 has two parts.

The **letter** is the generation, which NVIDIA calls an architecture and names after a scientist: A for Ampere, H for Hopper, L for Lovelace, B for Blackwell, with Rubin arriving in 2026 and Feynman promised for 2028. A new one comes out every year or two.

The **number** is the size within that generation. Bigger means more powerful and more expensive. The numbers do not follow much logic beyond that.

The letter matters more than it looks, because each generation adds features and not only speed. Hopper GPUs can calculate directly with 8-bit numbers. Blackwell goes down to 4 bits. That is what makes [quantization](/inference-engineering/quantization) pay off on real hardware.

One detail about Rubin caught my eye. It comes with a separate chip built for compute-heavy work like prefill. The split between reading and writing from the last chapter is now showing up in the hardware itself.

## You never rent only a GPU

In the cloud, the thing you rent is an **instance**: a virtual machine with one or more GPUs, plus CPUs, ordinary RAM, disk, a network connection and the wiring between the GPUs.

Any of those can turn out to be the slow part. The book's example is small and telling. The same A100 GPU comes in two physical forms, and one has five percent more memory bandwidth than the other. Two instances with the same GPU name are not always the same machine.

One of those other parts deserves a second look: the CPU. NVIDIA makes its own, called Grace, and sells it joined to its GPUs as a single unit. That is the G in names like GH200 and GB200. What matters for inference is the link between the two, which carries up to 900 GB/s, several times more than the usual connection between a CPU and a GPU.

Why would that matter? Because the ordinary RAM beside a GPU is far bigger than the GPU's own memory. That makes it a good place to park things that do not fit, such as [the caches from earlier requests](/inference-engineering/kv-cache#where-to-keep-it), and a fast link makes fetching them back quick. For the Rubin generation the CPU is called Vera.

## More than one GPU

Big models do not fit on one GPU, so GPUs are sold wired together. The standard unit is a **node**: eight GPUs in one box.

What matters is how fast those wires are compared with the memory inside a single GPU.

Hold on to this picture. It decides how a model can be split across GPUs, which is [chapter 11](/inference-engineering/many-gpus). Anything that makes GPUs talk to each other constantly has to live inside one node.

It also puts my laptop in its place. Its memory is about as fast as the cable between two datacenter machines, and about {count(3350 / membw.read_gbs)} times slower than the memory in one H100.

## Less than one GPU

The opposite problem exists too. A speech model with a couple of billion parameters cannot keep an H100 busy, and the leftover capacity is money wasted.

Large GPUs can be cut into as many as seven independent pieces, each with its own share of compute and memory. This is called a **multi-instance GPU**, or MIG. Several small models can then share one big, modern chip. According to the book that is often a better deal than giving each of them an older, weaker GPU of its own.

## Chips that are not from NVIDIA

NVIDIA dominates, but plenty of companies make chips for inference. What helped me was the book's observation that each one is betting on a single edge:

- **Memory bandwidth.** Cerebras and Groq build chips that fetch much faster, which attacks the decode limit directly. That is why they post very high tokens per second.
- **Power.** Some chips aim to do the same work for less electricity.
- **Integration.** Google's TPUs and Amazon's chips are built to work closely with their own clouds.

They also share a problem, and it is not the silicon. Nearly all inference software is written for NVIDIA's programming system, CUDA. A chip without it has to bring its own software for everything. That is the subject of [the next chapter](/inference-engineering/software-stack). The book adds two more hurdles that have nothing to do with design: making chips this complicated at all, and getting enough of them installed in datacenters to be worth building on.

## Running it on your own machine

The other alternative is not to use a datacenter at all. A model running on the user's own laptop or phone has no network delay, keeps their data on their device, and costs the developer nothing per request.

The limits are the obvious ones: far less power, heat, battery, and endless variety in what people own.

The book has one comparison I liked. A top consumer NVIDIA card has 32 GB of memory at 1,792 GB/s. Apple's largest desktop chip has 512 GB at 819 GB/s. Using the three numbers, you can read that directly: the Apple machine can hold enormous models and will write with them slowly, and the NVIDIA card can hold only small ones and will write with them quickly.

My own laptop sits far below both, which is exactly why GPT-2 is the model I can measure on it.

Mixture of Experts models are a help on a desk, the book points out. One person's request only wakes a fraction of the weights, so a big model of that kind is less work for a single user than its size suggests.

Phones are where most on-device inference happens today, and they are far more limited. Even an expensive phone struggles with a model of more than a billion or two parameters. What fits are small, focused jobs where a delay would be noticed: turning speech into text, reading text aloud, translating. Both phone platforms now ship their own tools for building this into an app.

The book's view is that this is not a contest. Small models and quick questions will run on the device in your hand, and the heavy work will stay in a datacenter.

## Where this leaves us

How much it can calculate, how much it can hold, how fast it can fetch. Compute sets the pace of reading. Bandwidth sets the pace of writing. Memory size decides what you are allowed to run at all. Everything else on the spec sheet is detail around those three.
