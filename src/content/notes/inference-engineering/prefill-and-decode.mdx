Two things about model APIs look odd until you know what is underneath. The first word of an answer takes noticeably longer than the words after it. And output tokens cost several times more than input tokens.

Both come from one fact. Reading and writing are different jobs for a model, and they are slow for different reasons.

## Two phases

A request goes through the model in two phases.

**Prefill** is the model reading your prompt. Every token of the prompt goes through the model together, in a single pass.

**Decode** is the model writing its answer. This is the loop from [the basics note](/notes/how-llms-work#the-loop): one pass through the model produces one token, the token is added to the end, and the model goes again.

So a prompt of 256 tokens costs one pass. An answer of 256 tokens costs 256 passes. That should make writing much slower than reading, and I wanted to see how much.

It is only {count(mid.write_s / mid.read_s)} times slower. The single pass that reads 256 tokens is much more work than a pass that writes one. So what makes a pass slow?

## What does one pass cost?

To push a token through the model, the machine has to multiply it by every weight. GPT-2 small has 124 million of them, about half a gigabyte. Every one of those numbers has to travel from memory to the processor, get used, and make room for the next.

That gives a pass two costs:

- **Fetching** the weights from memory.
- **Calculating** with them.

In prefill, the weights are fetched once and used on 256 tokens. Lots of calculating per fetch.

In decode, the weights are fetched once and used on a single token. Then they are fetched again for the next token. Very little calculating per fetch.

Every chip has a limit on each of these, and the two limits have names.

**Compute** is how many arithmetic operations the chip can do per second. It is measured in FLOPS, floating-point operations per second.

**Memory bandwidth** is how many bytes per second can be moved from memory to the processor.

Picture a kitchen. Compute is how fast the cook can chop. Memory bandwidth is how fast ingredients can be carried in from the pantry. If the cook stands waiting for the next crate, the kitchen is limited by carrying. If crates pile up next to the board, it is limited by chopping.

Prefill is the second kitchen. There is plenty to chop for every crate, so the cook is the limit. The book's term is **compute bound**.

Decode is the first kitchen. A whole pantry is carried in to make one sandwich. The carrying is the limit. That is **memory bound**.

How lopsided are the two kitchens? There is a way to put a number on it, and I come to it [further down](#where-do-the-two-limits-meet), once batching is on the table.

## Using the compute that sits idle

If decode leaves compute idle, the obvious move is to give it more to do for each trip through the weights. Fetch the weights once, and use them for several people's tokens.

That is batching. I ran the same decode step for one request, then two at once, then four, and so on.

Two things to notice. Each person gets fewer tokens per second as the batch grows, because a pass with more in it takes longer. That is the latency for throughput trade from [chapter 2](/inference-engineering/know-your-workload), in numbers. And the last rows fall off a cliff. At some batch size the cook becomes the limit after all, and adding more work only makes everything slower. On a two-core laptop that happens early. On a GPU it happens much later, but it does happen.

## Where do the two limits meet?

How much later? This can be worked out, and it is the one piece of real theory in this chapter.

Every chip has a ratio: how many arithmetic operations it can do in the time it takes to fetch one byte. The book calls it the ops-to-byte ratio. An H100 can do about 989 trillion operations a second on 16-bit numbers, and fetch 3,350 billion bytes a second. Divide one by the other and you get 295 operations for every byte.

Every job has a matching number: how many operations it needs for each byte it has to fetch. That one is called **arithmetic intensity**.

Put the two side by side. If the job needs fewer operations per byte than the chip can supply, the arithmetic finishes early and the chip waits for memory. That is memory bound. If it needs more, the bytes arrive and queue up for the arithmetic. That is compute bound.

So what does a pass through a language model need? Here is the roughest version of the sum. Take one weight, stored in 16 bits. That is two bytes to fetch. For each token going through, the weight takes part in one multiplication and one addition. Two operations for two bytes. So a pass needs about one operation per byte for each token in it.

- Decode for one person is one token per pass. It needs about 1 operation per byte, on a chip that can supply 295.
- Thirty-two people decoding together is 32 tokens per pass. It needs about 32.
- Prefill of a 2,000-token prompt needs about 2,000.

That is this whole chapter in one figure. Writing for one user keeps a third of one percent of the chip's arithmetic busy. Reading a long prompt uses all of it. And the two limits meet at a few hundred tokens per pass, which is why adding a few dozen requests to a batch is nearly free and adding thousands is not.

Real passes are messier than my sum. The book works a proper example for attention alone, counting every read, every write and every operation over a 4,096-token text, and arrives at 62 operations per byte against the H100's 295. That is a different number from mine and the same verdict: decode is memory bound. It also says, fairly, that this is an exercise for building intuition and not something inference engineers do every day.

The usual picture for all this is called a **roofline** chart: a sloping line where memory is the limit, meeting a flat line where compute is. The calculator above is that chart without the drawing.

Does the sum hold on my laptop? In its best run it did {count(compute.matmul_gflops)} billion operations a second on a large matrix multiplication, and earlier I measured it fetching {membw.read_gbs} GB a second. That is {count(laptopRatio)} operations per byte. GPT-2 is stored in 32-bit numbers, four bytes a weight, so each token in a pass needs half an operation per byte. The two limits should meet at about {count(laptopMeet)} requests per pass.

Now look back at the batching bars. Throughput did stop growing around there: it peaked at {best.batch} requests and fell at {biggest.batch}. So the sum finds the right neighbourhood. It also shows how far my setup is from the roof. A machine sitting exactly on both limits would write about {count(laptopRoof)} tokens a second at that point, and mine managed {best.total_tps}. The roof tells you where the limits are. It does not promise that your software reaches them. Closing that gap is what [chapter 7](/inference-engineering/software-stack) is about.

## What each phase decides

This gives the two numbers that the next chapter is about.

Prefill decides how long you wait for the **first** token. A longer prompt means more to read, so a longer wait. My laptop needed {span(ttft[0].ttft_ms / 1000)} to start answering a {ttft[0].prompt}-token prompt and {span(ttft[3].ttft_ms / 1000)} for a {count(ttft[3].prompt)}-token one.

Decode decides how quickly the **rest** arrive.

Decode also gets slower as the text gets longer. After the {ttft[0].prompt}-token prompt the laptop wrote {ttft[0].tps} tokens a second. After the {count(ttft[3].prompt)}-token one it wrote {ttft[3].tps}. Each new token has to look back at every earlier one, and there were more of them to look at. Attention is the one part of the model whose cost grows with the length of the text.

That growth is where a lot of research goes. Three ideas from the book are worth knowing by name:

- **FlashAttention** computes exactly the same attention, arranged so that intermediate results never leave the chip's fast on-board memory. Same answer, fewer trips to the pantry.
- **PagedAttention** stores what attention needs to remember in small fixed-size blocks, so memory can be handed out and reclaimed without leaving unusable gaps.
- Cheaper kinds of attention change the maths itself. Sliding window attention only looks back a fixed distance, often several thousand tokens. Others squeeze the older part of the text into a summary, or swap the exact calculation for an approximation. They give up a little quality, and they have to be built in when the model is trained.

A few models go a step further and replace attention in some of their layers with a different mechanism, one whose cost grows in a straight line with the length of the text. The book mentions Mamba as the best known, and says mixtures of the two kinds of layer are becoming more common.

What attention needs to remember is called the KV cache, and it gets [a chapter of its own](/inference-engineering/kv-cache).

## Two exceptions worth knowing

Not every model reads all of its weights for every token. A **Mixture of Experts** model splits its big layers into many smaller "experts" and sends each token through only a few. The choice is made again at every layer, by a tiny router inside the model. The book's example is a Qwen model with 235 billion parameters. Each of its layers has 128 experts, a token goes through 8 of them, and about 22 billion parameters end up being used for any one token. For a single user that is a large saving. On a busy server, different requests wake different experts, so in practice nearly all the weights stay in use.

And image and video models do not write token by token at all. They start from noise and refine the whole picture over a few dozen steps, with every step working on the entire image. That makes them compute bound, like prefill. They come back in [chapter 12](/inference-engineering/other-modalities).

## Where this leaves us

Reading is one heavy pass, limited by how fast the chip can calculate. Writing is many light passes, each limited by how fast the chip can fetch. Almost every technique later in this series is aimed at one of those two limits.
